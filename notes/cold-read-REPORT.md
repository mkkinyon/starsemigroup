Reader: a semigroup theorist / universal algebraist with no prior exposure to the project. Files read: CONTEXT.md, notes-embedding.tex (all of Sections 1, 2, 4, 5, 7; Section 3 and the Files paragraph skimmed), status-table.tex, K-catalogue.md, n4-README.md, conditions.in. Two identities used in the proofs (the projection-algebra identity (x\*y)\*(y\*z) = (x\*y)\*z and Lemma 1.1(b)'s supp(pq) = supp p ∩ supp q for commuting pairs) were spot-checked numerically on P(P_4) with the project's pmon.py; both hold.

## 1. The state of the problem as I understood it

The question is which finite regular \*-semigroups S admit a \*-embedding into some partition monoid P_X, and the working hypothesis, supported by everything found so far, is that the answer is decided by the projection algebra P(S) (the set of projections with the operations θ_p(q) = pqp). Groups and sandwich matrices impose nothing (Theorem 3.1: every finite completely simple regular \*-semigroup embeds), so the obstructions are "global": they concern how projections in different D-classes interact. Since the class of embeddable finite S is exactly the class of finite models of the quasi-identities valid in all P_X (Proposition 7.1), the programme is to find these quasi-identities, prove them combinatorially in P_X, and find minimal finite witnesses that violate them.

The current list has eight independent conditions. (Q1): projections with a common lower bound commute, i.e. up-sets {x : px = xp = p} are inverse. The triangle condition (six variables, Section 4): two commuting triples with meets w, z, each a_i commuting with b_i and three cross matchings a_i ⋈ b_{i+1}, force w = z; it is a "master" condition, implying every other matching-pattern condition in every projection algebra, and it is proved for all X. The remaining six are "wiring" conditions: an unclosed path of matched (friendly) projections a ⋈ c ⋈ b (or of length 3), auxiliary projections attached by commutation, order, or "seen alike / sees alike" equalities, and the conclusion that the path closes. Of these, (R′), (V), (P₁), (Q) carry hand proofs (with (R), (S), (P₂) as corollaries); (E₆), (E₁) are only verified in P(P_m) for m ≤ 7. Each proved condition has a minimal non-embeddable projection-algebra witness of order 6, 7, 8 or 9; two further order-8 algebras and seven order-9 algebras are candidates (no embedding into P(P_m) for m ≤ 8, all proper subalgebras embed). Sufficiency results exist for graph semigroups and layered graph semigroups ((Q1) + (S) suffice there) and by exhaustive computation up to order 6 (projection algebras) and order 8 (regular \*-semigroups: (Q1) alone suffices).

The October work (Section 5.13) found that the order-9 candidates do not need longer paths but more auxiliaries, that the conditions (E₁), (E₆) are 2-path conditions in disguise once the terms u = aba, v = bab are nameable, and produced an (R′)-shaped candidate (R″) plus a catalogue K0–K6 of 2-path conditions with up to three auxiliaries, all verified for m ≤ 6 only. The authors now propose to view all obstructions as "local transitivity" statements for the friendly relation, formulated through transports of restrictions along matched edges, and ask whether a uniform criterion, a finite basis, or decidability can be had.

The document communicates this state clearly to a newcomer; the one thing I had to reconstruct is which rows of the status table rest on which theorem, because the (Q) proof is load-bearing for three rows (see Section 2.6 below).

Step 4 ("plain classes contain no T(d)-block") is not proved as written. The first half, where B_j lies in an N(y)-block, is correct. The second half reads:

> "If B_j ⊆ supp y, a T(y)-block of B_j meets the last N(d)-block of the Λ_j-path if the path has length > 1; that block is an N(a)- and N(y)-block (or the hub of W's class, impossible), and a T(y)-block lies in a union of T(a)-blocks or in an N(a)-block which is a union of T(y)-blocks, so cannot meet an N(a) ∩ N(y)-block; hence B_j meets D_j directly, E_W lies in Δ_j, and a path in Δ_j from C_j to E_W, read backwards, lies in W and gives C_j ⊆ W again."

The "last N(d)-block of the Λ_j-path" is a d-block, hence a z-block in W, i.e. an N(c)-block which is an N(d)-block ("common" in the sense of Step 5). Nothing shown makes it a block of a: W is a union of N(a)-blocks and of c-blocks, and these interleave freely. I believe the error comes from the word "common" being used in two senses in this subsection: in Step 3 the four kinds of N(y)-blocks include "common blocks = N(a)-blocks which are N(b)-blocks", while in Step 5 "common" means N(c) ∩ N(d). The sentence treats a common block in the second sense as if it were one in the first. The subsequent "a path in Δ_j from C_j to E_W, read backwards, lies in W" also needs B_j ⊆ W (the Δ_j-path may pass through B_j, which in this case is exactly what has not been excluded). So the case "B_j ⊆ supp y" of Step 4 is open. I spent some time looking for a repair with the tools of Steps 2–3 (every a-block meeting D_j either meets it inside B_j or is an N(a) ∩ N(b) ∩ N(y)-block; B_j is a cut vertex of both Δ_j and Λ_j between W and the T-blocks) and did not find one; it may need the b-side symmetry (from the b-side every hub has E^{(b)}_G = D_G, nonempty) transported to the a-side through y. Note that the intermediate claims of a proof by contradiction cannot be tested on instances, so the "found and checked on all instances of P(P_4)" safeguard does not cover them.

### 2.9 Other points

Abstract: "four of which ... we prove in every P_X, so that their witnesses are the fifth to ninth minimal non-embeddable projection algebras, and a tenth, of order 9, follows". The ordinal count presupposes four earlier minimal non-embeddable algebras; the status table says the minimality of P(Q₀) is not checked, and the minimality of P₉ is not claimed anywhere. Say "further" rather than "fifth to ninth".

Section 7, Question 4: "For finite algebras a recursive basis of quasi-identities is equivalent to decidability of embeddability." Semi-decidability of embeddability needs that a finite algebra embeddable in some P(P_X) embeds in some P(P_m) with m finite. I do not see this stated or proved anywhere, and it is not obvious: restriction to a finite subset of X does not preserve products (connectivity through the middle layer can leave the subset), and P_X for infinite X is not an ultraproduct of finite ones. The same question underlies the practice of treating "verified for m ≤ 7" as evidence for "valid in all P_X" (which is harmless for hand proofs, since Lemma 1.1 holds for all X, but should be a stated lemma if it is true). A compression lemma — "if a finite P embeds in P(P_X) then it embeds in P(P_m) with m ≤ f(|P|)" — would settle both this and the decidability question in one stroke; the census data (m ≤ n − 1 up to n = 9) suggest it is true.

Terminology: "a-good" in Step 4 of Section 5.11 is used but never defined (it evidently means "an N(a)-block or a union of T(a)-blocks"). The two meanings of "common block" should be separated (see 2.6).

Evidence levels are otherwise kept apart carefully throughout (the Caveat in 5.8, the preamble of 5.13, the three-level legend of status-table.tex, the header of conditions.in). The only conflation is the one created by the (Q) gap.

## 3. Conceptual assessment

### 3.1 Is the organising principle sound?

As a description of the proved path-closing conditions it is accurate and useful: (R), (R′), (P₁), (Q) and the SAT part of (S) are all proved by the same engine — classify the blocks of each projection into finitely many kinds using the commutation and "seen alike" hypotheses (Lemma 1.1(a),(b)), show that adjacency in the relevant join respects kinds, and conclude that a T-block of one letter cannot reach a T-block of the same letter. The "transports of restrictions" language is the right name for the index pairs (λ(E), δ(E)) of Section 5.11 and for the maps φ, ψ, χ of Section 5.2. I would not call it wishful, but it has not yet produced a theorem, and two of the eight independent conditions ((V), triangle) and one of the seven order-9 candidates do not fit the "close a 2-path" template. The honest statement is: the path-closing conditions form a family with a common proof engine; whether the family has a uniform description is the open question, and (V) and the triangle condition are the test cases a uniform description must also cover (the notes already observe that (V) says θ_c(θ_b(t)) = θ_c(t) about restrictions, and that the triangle condition becomes, after Theorem 4.9, a statement about a transported family with w ⋈ z; so both can be phrased with transports, which is encouraging).

The most promising concrete form of the principle is not the random-diagram sampling of step (b) but the following: make the "kinds and transitions" engine itself the object. Each of the proofs is a finite transition system (kinds of blocks of each letter, allowed adjacencies) plus the claim that no path connects two T-blocks of the same letter. If one can define, for a wiring diagram, the transition system mechanically (as hubtypes.py does for the hub case of (S)), then validity of the quasi-identity becomes a finite check, and that is a criterion of exactly the kind asked for in Question 7.3(4). The hub case of (S) is the proof of concept; (R′) and (P₁) look amenable; (Q) is the hard case because of the hubs.

### 3.2 Known theories

Chained projection groupoids (East–Azeef Muhammed, arXiv:2301.04845; East–Gray–Azeef Muhammed–Ruškuc 2025). This is the theory the "descent" paragraph is reinventing, and it should be adopted explicitly. A regular \*-semigroup is a projection algebra P together with an ordered groupoid over P (morphisms p → q are the elements s with ss\* = p, s\*s = q) and an evaluation map from the chain groupoid C(P) (paths of friendly projections modulo the linked-pair relation) into it. "Transport of restrictions along a matched edge" is the groupoid action on down-sets; "holonomy of a closed path of matchings" is the image in the group H_p of a closed chain; the notes' observation that in P_X this holonomy is a permutation of T(p) is the statement that the chained projection groupoid of P_X has H_p ≅ Sym(T(p)) acting on [0,p] ≅ P(P_{T(p)}). Two things this buys. (i) A precise formulation of the embedding problem for a projection algebra P: P embeds in P(P_X) iff some projection-generated regular \*-semigroup with projection algebra P embeds in P_X (the subsemigroup generated by the image of P has projection algebra exactly P, since (p₁⋯p_k)(p₁⋯p_k)\* ∈ P), and every such semigroup is a quotient of the free projection-generated PG(P) of East–Azeef Muhammed. So the quasi-identities are shadows, in P, of constraints on which quotients of PG(P) have permutation-type holonomy compatible with the non-friendly θ's. (ii) A tool for Question 7.3(1) (is embeddability of S determined by P(S)?): enumerate the finite quotients of PG(P) for small embeddable P and test them; the East–Azeef Muhammed machinery describes these quotients by their groupoid data. I would expect the answer to be negative in general but I cannot exhibit an example.

Inductive/ordered groupoids and the Ehresmann–Schein–Nambooripad theorem are the ancestors of the above; the "presheaf of down-sets with restriction maps" of 5.13(d) is the ordered-groupoid restriction. This is a language, not a criterion.

Biordered sets. For regular \*-semigroups the biordered set is determined by the projections (Nambooripad–Pastijn, "Regular involution semigroups", 1985, and Imaoka's theorem as used in the notes), so the biordered-set viewpoint is a re-encoding of the projection algebra. The one notion I would import is the sandwich set S(p,q), which in P_X has a concrete block description; it may give a cleaner name for "the matched restriction" θ_p(q) ⋈ θ_q(p) of Lemma 4.10. I do not think Nambooripad's theory adds a tool beyond that.

Tolerances. The friendly relation is not a tolerance of the algebra (correctly observed), but it is a reflexive symmetric relation whose maximal cliques are the completely simple \*-subsemigroups (5.13(e)); the gluing of overlapping cliques is what the obstructions constrain. The tolerance literature (Chajda, Czédli; Herrmann's glued sums; "tolerance blocks") studies exactly "an algebra covered by blocks of a tolerance, glued along overlaps", but for lattices, and the gluing there is along a quotient lattice, which has no analogue here. I would treat this as an analogy only. The compatibility relation of inverse semigroup theory is on elements, not projections, and reduces to equality on idempotents; it is not the right analogue.

Representable relation algebras, Hirsch–Hodkinson games. This is the most relevant analogy with real content. The class of representable involuted semigroups is a quasivariety (Schein) with an infinite, recursive, non-finitely-axiomatisable base; RRA likewise (Monk). Hirsch–Hodkinson's representation games give (a) an explicit recursive axiomatisation by the n-round game conditions and (b) the standard method ("rainbow" constructions) for proving non-finite-axiomatisability. The partition-monoid analogue is natural: a network is a finite set of points with a partial labelled partition for each projection of P; ∀ picks p, q and a point where θ_p(q) is not yet realised; ∃ adds points or merges classes. Winning the ω-game is representability (for finite P, with the finite-X caveat of 2.9), and the n-round conditions are universal sentences in the language of P, which by Proposition 7.1 can be replaced by quasi-identities. The SAT searches in P(P_m) are finite approximations of this game. Setting the game up would give the "parametrised basis" the authors conjecture, and the rainbow method is the route to a non-finite-basis theorem. On decidability: Hirsch–Jackson (JSL 2012) prove undecidability of representability of finite algebras for several signatures containing composition; I do not remember with certainty whether the pure {·, \*} signature is among them, and in any case the P_X problem is a different representation class (relations that are Boolean subalgebras of 2^X × 2^X, Proposition 7.2), so neither decidability nor undecidability transfers. The notes' Theorem 5.8 shows the obvious graph encodings collapse to a finite forbidden-subgraph condition, so an undecidability proof, if one exists, would have to use the layered or multi-auxiliary structure; Jackson–Volkov's adjacency semigroups with reversion ("The algebra of adjacency patterns: Rees matrix semigroups with reversion", 2010) are the closest existing encoding technique and are essentially the Q(G,⊥) of Section 5.1 — worth reading for both the finite-basis and the decidability questions.

Finite basis for quasivarieties. The relevant general fact is elementary: if the quasivariety generated by {P(P_X)} had a basis of quasi-identities in ≤ n variables, membership of a finite P would be decided by its n-generated subalgebras. So a non-finite-basis theorem needs an infinite family of minimal non-embeddable algebras P_k with all n-generated subalgebras embeddable for k ≫ n. Nothing in the data yet gives such a family: the witnesses have orders 6–9 and are explained by conditions with 4–7 variables. The observation that the number of auxiliaries grows with the order (one at 7, two at 8, three plus u, v at 9) is suggestive but the family is not there. The matching-pattern conditions are a warning in the other direction: an apparently infinite family (C_ℓ for all ℓ) collapsed to one 6-variable condition. Sapir's and Jackson's results on quasi-identities of finite semigroups do not apply directly (the generating class is infinite), but Jackson's "flat algebras" translation of universal Horn classes could be the way to encode a known non-finitely-based quasivariety.

### 3.3 Two cheap theoretical remarks

First, the bound "smallest counterexample between 9 and 19" can probably be sharpened: if p ⋈ q with p ≠ q then pq and qp are distinct non-projections determined by (p, q), so |S| ≥ |P(S)| + 2·(number of matched pairs). For 𝔓 this gives 14, for P₇ 17 (five matched pairs; T₁₉ has 19 elements), for the order-8 witnesses ≥ 20 and the order-9 candidates ≥ 21. Hence a counterexample of order ≤ 13 would have an embeddable projection algebra, i.e. would answer Question 7.3(1) negatively. This turns the "smallest counterexample" question into a sharper experiment (see 4).

Second, the finite-X lemma of 2.9 should be stated and proved (or stated as open); it is the hinge between the computer evidence and the theorems.

## 4. The candidate next steps

(a) Check whether P₈⁴, P₈⁵ and the order-9 (R′)-witness violate 2-path conditions. Cheap (a script run) and worth doing, but it is not decisive either way: even a positive outcome leaves (V), the triangle condition and candidate 2 outside the template. Do it, together with P₈⁷ and P(Q₀) as controls, but do not plan around the result.

(b) Reformulate the search language as transports of restrictions and sample random wiring diagrams. The reformulation is right (3.1), but random sampling of diagrams "to look for a criterion" is open-ended and duplicates what pa_sat already does for concrete algebras. I would replace it by the targeted version in 3.1: formalise the kinds-and-transitions engine for a diagram and try to make validity a finite check. That is a research step, not a search step, and should come after (c).

(c) A hand proof of (R″) or of K6. High value, and the first thing to do after fixing (Q): (R″) is the cleanest new condition and the closest relative of (R′), so it is the sharpest test of whether the (R′) proof is uniform. I would aim directly at the four-member family {(R′), (R″), and the two variants with θ_x(b) ≤ a, θ_x(b) ≤ b} and look for a single proof; the two variants are valid for m ≤ 5 without witnesses, which is exactly the situation in which a uniform proof is informative. K6 (no commutation atom at all, three "seen" equalities) is the better test of whether the engine needs commutation; try it second.

(d) SAT verification of (R″) and K0–K6 at m = 7. Zero intellectual cost, hours of machine time; start it now and let it run. It cannot raise the evidence level to "proved", but a failure at m = 7 would save a wasted hand proof, which is the main reason to run it before (c).

(e) The order-10 census. I would drop it in this form. Weeks of CPU would produce more candidates of the kind nobody can currently prove, and the yield per candidate is low (the order-9 batch produced one clean condition, (R″)). The one thing order 10 could give that smaller orders cannot is the start of an infinite family for a non-finite-basis theorem, and that is better obtained by targeted extension: extend only the known witnesses (P₈¹…P₈⁷, the seven order-9 candidates) by one or two points and look for algebras that violate nothing known. This is a few minutes per base algebra with extend.py.

(f) Theory. Yes, but in a specific order: first the finite-X / compression lemma (it underpins the decidability question, Question 7.3(4), and the status of all computer evidence); second the game formulation (gives the parametrised basis and the standard NFB method); third Question 7.3(1) via PG(P) quotients. "Possible non-finite-basis theorem" needs the infinite family first, which neither (e) nor theory alone will supply.
