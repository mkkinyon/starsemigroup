# Two inversions on a regular $*$-semigroup — summary

Throughout, $S$ is a regular $*$-semigroup, $+$ is a second involutory antiautomorphic inversion, and $\phi\colon x\mapsto x^{*+}$ (apply $*$, then $+$) is the resulting automorphism, of order $k$ when $S$ is finite. $P^*$ denotes the set of $*$-projections ($e=e^2=e^*$) and $P^+$ the set of $+$-projections.

## Short answer

**The conjecture is false as stated, but true after one correction; the correction is forced and is itself easy to describe.**

* **Counterexample.** Let $A=\{1,2,3,4,5\}$, $S=A\times A$, $(x,y)^*=(y,x)$ and $(x,y)^+=(y\beta,x\beta^{-1})$ with $\beta=(1\,2)(3\,4\,5)$. Then $\phi(x,y)=(x\beta,y\beta^{-1})$ has order $k=\operatorname{lcm}(2,3)=6$, but $|S|=25<36$, so $S$ contains no square band $A'\times A'$ with $|A'|=6$ at all. (The trouble is simply that $k$ is a least common multiple.) See `05-counterexamples.md`.

* **Corrected statement (proved in `03-main-theorem.md`).** Define, for $e\in P^*$,
$$\rho(e) \;=\; e^{+*}e^{+},$$
the unique $*$-projection $\mathcal L$-related to $e^+$. Then $\rho$ is a permutation of $P^*$, and:
  1. $\rho(e)=e \iff e^+=e$; hence $\rho=\mathrm{id}\iff {*}={+}$.
  2. $k$ equals the order of the permutation $\rho$, i.e. the lcm of the lengths of its cycles.
  3. For every cycle $(e_0\,e_1\,\dots\,e_{m-1})$ of $\rho$ with $m\ge 2$, the set
  $$E=\{e_ae_b : 0\le a,b<m\}$$
  is a square band, $e_ae_b\mapsto(a,b)$ being an isomorphism $E\cong\mathbb Z_m\times\mathbb Z_m$; it is closed under both inversions, with $(e_ae_b)^*=e_be_a$ and $(e_ae_b)^+=e_{b-1}e_{a+1}$ (indices mod $m$). These are distinct inversions on $E$, and $\phi|_E$ has order exactly $m$.

  So: *$S$ contains a square band $A\times A$ on which $*$ and $+$ restrict to distinct inversions, with $|A|=m$ for every cycle length $m\ge2$ of $\rho$, and $k=\operatorname{lcm}$ of these $m$'s.* In particular the original conjecture holds verbatim whenever $k$ is a prime power (then some cycle has length exactly $k$), so in particular for $k=2$ and $k$ prime.

* **Exactly when the original conjecture holds.** A $*$,$+$-closed square band $A\times A$ with $|A|=k$ and distinct restrictions exists iff there is a union $B$ of $\rho$-cycles with $|B|=k$, $\rho|_B\ne\mathrm{id}$, such that $\{ef: e,f\in B\}$ is a band (`03-main-theorem.md`, Remark). Each single cycle always works; unions of cycles need not.

* **The case $k=2$** has a completely coordinate-free three-line proof valid for *arbitrary* (not necessarily finite) regular $*$-semigroups, and confirms your hint: the bad $2\times2$ band lies in the $\{\cdot,*,+\}$-subalgebra generated by any $a$ with $a^*\ne a^+$. See `04-case-k-equals-2.md`.

# 2. Preliminaries and the Rees-matrix normal form

**Terminology.** Throughout these notes, *inversion* is shorthand for *involutory antiautomorphic inversion*: a map $x\mapsto x^\dagger$ with $x^{\dagger\dagger}=x$, $(xy)^\dagger=y^\dagger x^\dagger$ and $xx^\dagger x=x$. All three properties are used in every structure statement (Lemma B's formula comes from $xx^+x=x$, condition $(\ast)$ from anti-automorphy, and involutivity is what makes ${*}\circ{+}$ an automorphism whose inverse is ${+}\circ{*}$), and the brute-force searches in the scripts enforce all three. A mere choice of inverses $x\mapsto x'\in V(x)$ is never meant; see §7 for why the two notions behave very differently.

## 2.1 Standard facts

Let $(S,*)$ be a regular $*$-semigroup. Applying $*$ to $xx^*x=x$ gives $x^*xx^*=x^*$, so $x^*\in V(x)$ (the set of inverses of $x$). Consequently $x\,\mathcal R\,xx^*$, $x\,\mathcal L\,x^*x$, and $x^*\in R_{x^*x}\cap L_{xx^*}$; also $x^*\,\mathcal D\,x$.

**Fact 1.** *Each $\mathcal R$-class and each $\mathcal L$-class of $S$ contains exactly one $*$-projection.* Existence: $xx^*\in R_x$ and $x^*x\in L_x$. Uniqueness: if $e,f\in P^*$ and $e\,\mathcal R\,f$ then $e=fe$, $f=ef$, so $e=e^*=(fe)^*=e^*f^*=ef=f$.

**Fact 2.** *Two inverses of $x$ lying in the same $\mathcal H$-class are equal* (Green's lemma / the standard description of $V(x)$).

Now let $+$ be a second inversion. Both $x^*$ and $x^+$ are inverses of $x$.

**Fact 3.** *If $P^*\subseteq P^+$ then ${*}={+}$.* Indeed $P^*$ and $P^+$ are both transversals of the $\mathcal R$-classes (Fact 1), so $P^*\subseteq P^+$ forces $P^*=P^+$. Then $xx^*$ and $xx^+$ are $+$-projections in $R_x$, hence equal, and likewise $x^*x=x^+x$; so $x^*,x^+$ are inverses of $x$ in the same $\mathcal H$-class $R_{x^*x}\cap L_{xx^*}$, hence equal by Fact 2.

So if ${*}\ne{+}$, some $e\in P^*$ satisfies $e^+\ne e$; more precisely, if $a^*\ne a^+$ then at least one of $aa^*$, $a^*a$ is not $+$-fixed (the argument of Fact 3 applied to $x=a$).

**Fact 4.** With $\phi(x)=x^{*+}$ (an automorphism, being a composite of two anti-automorphisms) we have $x^+=\phi(x^*)$ and $\phi^{-1}(x)=x^{+*}$. For $e\in P^*$: $\phi(e)=e^+$ and $\phi^{-1}(e)=e^{+*}$, and both lie in $V(e)$ — the first because $+$ is an inversion, the second because an anti-automorphism maps $V(e)$ onto $V(e^*)=V(e)$. Both are idempotents (anti-automorphisms preserve idempotents).

## 2.2 Reduction to one $\mathcal D$-class (finite case)

Assume $S$ finite and ${*}\ne{+}$. Since $*$ and $+$ preserve $\mathcal D$-classes, there is a $\mathcal D$-class $D$ with ${*}|_D\ne{+}|_D$. Both $*$ and $+$ map the ideals $J(x)=S^1xS^1$ and $I(x)=J(x)\setminus D$ ($x\in D$) to themselves, so both induce inversions on the principal factor $D^0=D\cup\{0\}$, which is completely 0-simple ($S$ finite, $D$ regular). A rectangular band inside $D^0$ not containing $0$ is a rectangular band inside $S$ with the same multiplication (a rectangular band containing $0$ is $\{0\}$, since $x0x=x$ forces $x=0$). So it suffices to prove everything for completely 0-simple $S$; the completely simple case is included (no zero, or ignore it).

## 2.3 Normal form for one inversion

Let $S=\mathcal M^0[G;I,\Lambda;P]$ be completely 0-simple with an inversion $*$. Write $H_{i\lambda}$ for the $\mathcal H$-class $\{(i,g,\lambda)\}$; it is a group iff $p_{\lambda i}\ne0$. By Fact 1 there is a bijection $\sigma\colon I\to\Lambda$ such that the $*$-projection of $R_i$ lies in $H_{i,\sigma i}$. For $x\in H_{i\lambda}$ we have $x^*\in R_{x^*x}\cap L_{xx^*}$, where $xx^*$ is the projection of $R_i$ (so $L_{xx^*}=L_{\sigma i}$) and $x^*x$ is the projection of $L_\lambda$ (so $R_{x^*x}=R_{\sigma^{-1}\lambda}$). Hence
$$x\in H_{i\lambda}\ \Longrightarrow\ x^*\in H_{\sigma^{-1}\lambda,\ \sigma i}.$$
Re-index $\Lambda$ by $I$ via $\sigma$, so that $\sigma=\mathrm{id}$, $p_{ii}\ne0$, and (after the Rees normalisation $(i,g,\lambda)\mapsto(i,gp_{\lambda\lambda},\lambda)$, which replaces $p_{\lambda j}$ by $p_{\lambda\lambda}^{-1}p_{\lambda j}$) $p_{ii}=1$ for all $i$.

Now $(i,g,j)^*=(j,f_{ij}(g),i)$ for some bijections $f_{ij}$. The inversion identity gives $(i,g,j)=(i,gp_{jj}f_{ij}(g)p_{ii}g,j)=(i,gf_{ij}(g)g,j)$, so $f_{ij}(g)=g^{-1}$:
$$(i,g,j)^*=(j,g^{-1},i).$$
Anti-automorphy: for $p_{jk}\ne0$, $\big((i,g,j)(k,h,l)\big)^*=(l,h^{-1}p_{jk}^{-1}g^{-1},i)$ must equal $(l,h^{-1},k)(j,g^{-1},i)=(l,h^{-1}p_{kj}g^{-1},i)$, and for $p_{jk}=0$ the right side must be $0$. Hence

**Lemma A.** *A completely 0-simple regular $*$-semigroup is, up to isomorphism, $\mathcal M^0[G;I,I;P]$ with $p_{ii}=1$, $p_{ji}=p_{ij}^{-1}$ (in $G^0$, with $p_{ij}=0\iff p_{ji}=0$), and $(i,g,j)^*=(j,g^{-1},i)$. Its $*$-projections are $e_i:=(i,1,i)$, and the idempotent of a group $\mathcal H$-class $H_{ij}$ is $e_{ij}:=(i,p_{ij},j)=e_ie_j$.*

(The converse is a direct check. Note $e_ie_j=(i,p_{ij},j)$ is idempotent whenever $p_{ij}\ne0$: $(i,p_{ij}p_{ji}p_{ij},j)=(i,p_{ij},j)$.)

## 2.4 All inversions on such an $S$

Keep the coordinates of Lemma A and let $+$ be any inversion on $S$. By the same reasoning there is a permutation $\rho$ of $I$ with the $+$-projection of $R_i$ in $H_{i,\rho i}$ (so $p_{\rho i,i}\ne0$), and $x\in H_{ij}\Rightarrow x^+\in H_{\rho^{-1}j,\rho i}$. Writing $(i,g,j)^+=(\rho^{-1}j,f(g),\rho i)$, the inversion identity $(i,g,j)(\rho^{-1}j,f(g),\rho i)(i,g,j)=(i,\,g\,p_{j,\rho^{-1}j}\,f(g)\,p_{\rho i,i}\,g,\,j)$ forces
$$(i,g,j)^+=\big(\rho^{-1}j,\ q_j^{-1}\,g^{-1}\,q_{\rho i}^{-1},\ \rho i\big),\qquad q_j:=p_{j,\rho^{-1}j}\ (\ne0).$$
This map is automatically an involution. Comparing $\big((i,g,j)(k,h,l)\big)^+=(\rho^{-1}l,\ q_l^{-1}h^{-1}p_{jk}^{-1}g^{-1}q_{\rho i}^{-1},\ \rho i)$ with
$(k,h,l)^+(i,g,j)^+=(\rho^{-1}l,\ q_l^{-1}h^{-1}\,q_{\rho k}^{-1}\,p_{\rho k,\rho^{-1}j}\,q_j^{-1}\,g^{-1}q_{\rho i}^{-1},\ \rho i)$ and using $q_{\rho k}=p_{\rho k,k}$, $p_{jk}^{-1}=p_{kj}$:

**Lemma B.** *With $S$ as in Lemma A, the inversions of $S$ are in bijection with the permutations $\rho$ of $I$ satisfying $p_{\rho i,i}\ne0$ for all $i$ and*
$$p_{\rho k,\,\rho^{-1}j}\;=\;p_{\rho k,\,k}\;p_{kj}\;p_{j,\,\rho^{-1}j}\qquad\text{for all }j,k\in I\quad(\ast)$$
*(an identity in $G^0$; in particular $p_{\rho k,\rho^{-1}j}=0\iff p_{kj}=0$). The inversion attached to $\rho$ is the formula displayed above; $\rho=\mathrm{id}$ gives $*$. Its projections are the idempotents $e_{i,\rho i}$; in terms of Fact 1, $\rho$ is the map $e_i\mapsto e_{\rho i}$, i.e.* $\rho(e)=e^{+*}e^{+}$ *(the $*$-projection $\mathcal L$-related to $e^+$), which is how $\rho$ was defined coordinate-free in the summary.*

Consistency check with your square-band example: $G$ trivial, $(\ast)$ is vacuous, every permutation $\rho$ of $I$ qualifies, giving $|I|!$ inversions, and the formula reads $(i,j)^+=(\rho^{-1}j,\rho i)$, i.e. your $(x,y)^+=(y\alpha,x\alpha^{-1})$ with $\alpha=\rho^{-1}$.

Finally, in these coordinates
$$\phi(i,g,j)=\big((i,g,j)^*\big)^+=(j,g^{-1},i)^+=\big(\rho^{-1}i,\ p_{\rho^{-1}i,\,i}\;g\;p_{j,\,\rho j},\ \rho j\big).\qquad(\dagger)$$

# 3. The main theorem

Coordinates as in Lemmas A and B of `02-preliminaries.md`: $S=\mathcal M^0[G;I,I;P]$, $p_{ii}=1$, $p_{ji}=p_{ij}^{-1}$, $(i,g,j)^*=(j,g^{-1},i)$, and $+$ corresponds to a permutation $\rho$ of $I$ satisfying $(\ast)$. Write $e_i=(i,1,i)$ for the $*$-projections and $e_{ij}=(i,p_{ij},j)=e_ie_j$.

## 3.1 Orbit flatness

Call a subset $B\subseteq I$ **flat** if $p_{ij}\ne0$ and $p_{ij}p_{jl}=p_{il}$ for all $i,j,l\in B$. (Equivalently the $B\times B$ block of $P$ is $p_{ij}=c_ic_j^{-1}$ for some $c\colon B\to G$; equivalently the idempotents $\{e_{ij}: i,j\in B\}$ form a band, see 3.3.)

**Lemma C (orbits are flat).** *Every $\rho$-orbit $B=\{i_0,i_1=\rho i_0,\dots,i_{m-1}\}$ is flat.*

*Proof.* Write $p_{ab}$ for $p_{i_ai_b}$ with indices mod $m$, and $d_a:=p_{a,a+1}$; note $d_a\ne0$ because $p_{\rho i,i}\ne0$ for all $i$. Put $k=i_a$, $j=i_{b+1}$ in $(\ast)$: $\rho k=i_{a+1}$, $\rho^{-1}j=i_b$, so
$$p_{a+1,b}=p_{a+1,a}\,p_{a,b+1}\,p_{b+1,b},\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad p_{a,b+1}=d_a\;p_{a+1,b}\;d_b\quad\text{(in }G^0).\tag{$\ast\ast$}$$
Claim: $p_{a,a+n}=d_ad_{a+1}\cdots d_{a+n-1}$ for all $a$ and all $n\ge0$ (empty product $=1$). For $n=0,1$ this is $p_{aa}=1$ and the definition of $d_a$. For $n\ge1$, $(\ast\ast)$ with $b=a+n$ gives $p_{a,a+n+1}=d_a\,p_{a+1,(a+1)+(n-1)}\,d_{a+n}=d_a(d_{a+1}\cdots d_{a+n-1})d_{a+n}$ by induction. In particular every $p_{ab}$ is nonzero, and for $b=a+n_1$, $c=b+n_2$ ($n_1,n_2\ge0$) we get $p_{ab}p_{bc}=(d_a\cdots d_{b-1})(d_b\cdots d_{c-1})=p_{a,a+n_1+n_2}=p_{ac}$. $\square$

(Taking $n=m$ shows $d_ad_{a+1}\cdots d_{a+m-1}=1$: the holonomy around the cycle is trivial.)

## 3.2 Consequences for $\phi$

By $(\dagger)$ of `02-preliminaries.md`, $\phi(i,g,j)=(\rho^{-1}i,\;p_{\rho^{-1}i,i}\,g\,p_{j,\rho j},\;\rho j)$. Iterating and using Lemma C to telescope ($p_{\rho^{-2}i,\rho^{-1}i}\,p_{\rho^{-1}i,i}=p_{\rho^{-2}i,i}$, all indices in one orbit):
$$\phi^n(i,g,j)=\big(\rho^{-n}i,\ p_{\rho^{-n}i,\,i}\;g\;p_{j,\,\rho^nj},\ \rho^nj\big)\qquad(n\ge0).$$
Hence $\phi^n=\mathrm{id}$ on $S$ iff $\rho^n=\mathrm{id}$ (if $\rho^n=\mathrm{id}$ the middle entry is $p_{ii}gp_{jj}=g$). So **the order of $\phi$ on a completely 0-simple regular $*$-semigroup equals the order of $\rho$**, the lcm of its cycle lengths; the group $G$ contributes nothing.

## 3.3 The orbit bands

**Proposition D.** *Let $B=\{i_0,\dots,i_{m-1}\}$ be a $\rho$-orbit with $m\ge2$ and put $E_B=\{e_{i_ai_b}:0\le a,b<m\}=\{e_{i_a}e_{i_b}\}$. Then:*

1. *$E_B$ is a square band: $e_{i_ai_b}\mapsto(a,b)$ is an isomorphism $E_B\to\mathbb Z_m\times\mathbb Z_m$.*
2. *$E_B$ is closed under $*$ and $+$, with $e_{i_ai_b}^*=e_{i_bi_a}$ and $e_{i_ai_b}^+=e_{i_{b-1}i_{a+1}}$.*
3. *On $E_B$ the two inversions are distinct, and $\phi|_{E_B}$ has order exactly $m$.*

*Proof.* (1) By Lemma C, $e_{i_ai_b}e_{i_ci_d}=(i_a,\,p_{ab}\,p_{bc}\,p_{cd},\,i_d)=(i_a,p_{ad},i_d)=e_{i_ai_d}$, and the $m^2$ elements lie in distinct $\mathcal H$-classes. (2) $(i_a,p_{ab},i_b)^*=(i_b,p_{ab}^{-1},i_a)=(i_b,p_{ba},i_a)$. For $+$, Lemma B gives $(i_a,p_{ab},i_b)^+=(i_{b-1},\ q_{i_b}^{-1}p_{ab}^{-1}q_{i_{a+1}}^{-1},\ i_{a+1})$ with $q_{i_b}=p_{b,b-1}$ and $q_{i_{a+1}}=p_{a+1,a}$, so the middle entry is $p_{b-1,b}\,p_{ba}\,p_{a,a+1}=p_{b-1,a+1}$ by flatness. (3) In the coordinates of (1), $*$ is $(a,b)\mapsto(b,a)$ and $+$ is $(a,b)\mapsto(b-1,a+1)$; they differ as $m\ge2$. Their composite $\phi$ is $(a,b)\mapsto(a-1,b+1)$, of order $m$. $\square$

## 3.4 Main theorem

**Theorem.** *Let $S$ be a finite regular $*$-semigroup, $+$ a second inversion with ${+}\ne{*}$, $\phi(x)=x^{*+}$ of order $k$, and $\rho\colon P^*\to P^*$, $\rho(e)=e^{+*}e^{+}$. Then $\rho$ is a permutation of $P^*$ preserving $\mathcal D$-classes, $\rho(e)=e\iff e^+=e$, and*

1. *$k$ is the order of $\rho$ (the lcm of its cycle lengths);*
2. *for every cycle $(e_0\,e_1\cdots e_{m-1})$ of $\rho$ with $m\ge2$, $E=\{e_ae_b\}$ is a square band $\cong\mathbb Z_m\times\mathbb Z_m$ closed under $*$ and $+$, on which $*$ and $+$ restrict to distinct inversions ($(e_ae_b)^*=e_be_a$, $(e_ae_b)^+=e_{b-1}e_{a+1}$) and on which $\phi$ has order exactly $m$;*
3. *at least one cycle of length $m\ge2$ exists; the set of such $m$ has lcm $k$; and $E\subseteq\langle e_0\rangle_{\cdot,*,+}$.*

*Proof.* Work one $\mathcal D$-class $D$ at a time (§2.2). On $D^0$ in the coordinates of Lemma A, $e_i\mapsto e_{\rho i}$ is the map $e\mapsto e^{+*}e^+$ (Lemma B), so $\rho$ is a permutation of $P^*\cap D$; $\rho(e_i)=e_i$ iff $e_i^+\in H_{ii}$ iff $e_i^+=e_i$ ($e_i^+$ is an idempotent and $H_{ii}$ contains only one). If ${*}={+}$ on $D$ then $\phi|_D=\mathrm{id}$ and $\rho|_D=\mathrm{id}$; otherwise $\rho|_D\ne\mathrm{id}$ by Fact 3 (applied to $D^0$). (1) is §3.2 together with $k=\operatorname{lcm}_D\operatorname{ord}(\phi|_D)$. (2) is Proposition D. For (3), some $D$ has ${*}|_D\ne{+}|_D$, so $\rho$ has a nontrivial cycle; the lcm statement is (1); and $e_{a+1}=\rho(e_a)=e_a^{+*}e_a^{+}$, so the whole cycle, and hence $E$, lies in the subalgebra generated by $e_0$. $\square$

**Corollary.** *The original conjecture (a $*$,$+$-closed square band $A\times A$ with $|A|=k$ and distinct restrictions) holds whenever $k$ is a prime power — in particular for $k=2$ and for $k$ prime — because then some cycle of $\rho$ has length exactly $k$.*

## 3.5 Remark: exactly when $|A|=k$ is attainable

Let $E\cong A\times A$ be any square band in $S$ closed under $*$ and $+$ on which they differ. $E$ lies in a single $\mathcal D$-class $D$ (it is a simple subsemigroup, and $\mathcal D=\mathcal J$). Distinct idempotents of $E$ are in distinct $\mathcal H$-classes of $S$, and two elements of $E$ that are $\mathcal L$-related in $E$ but in different rows cannot be $\mathcal R$-related in $S$; so the rows of $E$ are $|A|$ distinct $\mathcal R$-classes $B\subseteq I$ of $D$ and the columns are $|A|$ distinct $\mathcal L$-classes $C$. Since $*$ maps $H_{ij}\to H_{ji}$, $C=B$; since $+$ maps $H_{ij}\to H_{\rho^{-1}j,\rho i}$, $\rho B=B$. Each $H_{ij}$ ($i,j\in B$) is a group $\mathcal H$-class containing exactly one idempotent, $e_{ij}$, so $E=\{e_{ij}:i,j\in B\}$ and the band condition $e_{ij}e_{jl}=e_{il}$ says $B$ is flat. Finally ${*}\ne{+}$ on $E$ iff $\rho|_B\ne\mathrm{id}$. Conversely every flat $\rho$-invariant $B$ with $\rho|_B\ne\mathrm{id}$ gives such an $E$ (same computation as Proposition D). Therefore:

> *A $*$,$+$-closed square band $A\times A$ with $|A|=k$ and distinct restrictions exists iff some $\mathcal D$-class contains a union $B$ of $\rho$-cycles with $|B|=k$, $\rho|_B\ne\mathrm{id}$, and $B$ flat.*

Single cycles are always flat (Lemma C); unions of cycles need not be, and there need not be a union of the right size at all. Both failure modes occur in `05-counterexamples.md`.

# 4. The case $k=2$, coordinate-free (no finiteness needed)

Let $(S,*)$ be any regular $*$-semigroup and $+$ a second inversion with ${+}\ne{*}$ and $\phi^2=\mathrm{id}$, i.e. $x^{*+*+}=x$, equivalently $x^{*+*}=x^{+}$ for all $x$.

**Step 1: a $*$-projection moved by $+$.** By Fact 3 of `02-preliminaries.md` there is $e\in P^*$ with $f:=e^+\ne e$. (If $a^*\ne a^+$, one of $e=aa^*$ or $e=a^*a$ works, so everything below happens inside the subalgebra generated by $a$ — as you suggested.)

**Step 2: $f$ is again a $*$-projection.** $f$ is idempotent (anti-automorphisms preserve idempotents) and
$$f^*=e^{+*}=e^{*+*}=e^{+}=f,$$
using $e^*=e$ and $x^{*+*}=x^+$. Also $f=e^+\in V(e)$, so
$$efe=e,\qquad fef=f.$$

**Step 3: $\{e,ef,fe,f\}$ is a $2\times2$ square band.** Using only $e^2=e$, $f^2=f$, $efe=e$, $fef=f$:

| $\cdot$ | $e$ | $ef$ | $fe$ | $f$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $e$ | $e$ | $ef$ | $e$ | $ef$ |
| $ef$ | $e$ | $ef$ | $e$ | $ef$ |
| $fe$ | $fe$ | $f$ | $fe$ | $f$ |
| $f$ | $fe$ | $f$ | $fe$ | $f$ |

(e.g. $ef\cdot ef=(efe)f=ef$, $fe\cdot ef=f(ee)f=fef=f$, $ef\cdot fe=efe=e$). With $e\mapsto(1,1)$, $ef\mapsto(1,2)$, $fe\mapsto(2,1)$, $f\mapsto(2,2)$ this is exactly the table of $\{1,2\}\times\{1,2\}$. The four elements are distinct: $ef=e$ would give $fe=(ef)^*=e^*=e$ and then $f=fef=(fe)f=ef=e$; $ef=f$ would give $fe=f$ and $e=efe=(ef)e=fe=f$; $ef=fe$ would give $e=efe=(fe)e=fe=ef$, reducing to the first case.

**Step 4: closure and distinctness.** $(ef)^*=f^*e^*=fe$, so $*$ is the swap $(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)$. And $e^+=f$, $f^+=e^{++}=e$, $(ef)^+=f^+e^+=ef$, $(fe)^+=fe$, so $+$ is $(x,y)\mapsto(y\alpha,x\alpha^{-1})$ with $\alpha=(1\,2)$. These are the two distinct inversions of the $2\times2$ band. $\square$

**Remarks.**

* Nothing here used finiteness, so for $k=2$ the conjecture holds for all regular $*$-semigroups.
* The same argument works for any $2$-cycle $\{e,f\}$ of $\rho$ (see the main theorem), for arbitrary $k$: a $2$-cycle means $\phi^2(e)=e$, and Step 2 only needs $f^*=\phi^{-1}(e)=\phi(e)=f$.
* Compare with the general proof: in Rees coordinates $e=e_i$, $f=e_j$ with $\rho=(i\,j)$ on $\{i,j\}$, and the band is $\{e_{ii},e_{ij},e_{ji},e_{jj}\}$. A $2$-point block $\begin{pmatrix}1&p\\p^{-1}&1\end{pmatrix}$ is automatically flat, which is why no analogue of Lemma C is needed when $m=2$; for $m\ge3$ flatness of the orbit is a genuine consequence of $(\ast)$ and is where the work lies.

# 5. Counterexamples to "$|A|=k$", and the computer check

## 5.1 A single square band

$A=\{1,2,3,4,5\}$, $S=A\times A$, $(x,y)^*=(y,x)$, $(x,y)^+=(y\beta,x\beta^{-1})$ with $\beta=(1\,2)(3\,4\,5)$.

* $\phi(x,y)=\big((y,x)\big)^+=(x\beta,y\beta^{-1})$, so $\phi^n=\mathrm{id}$ iff $\beta^n=1$, i.e. $k=6$.
* A square band $A'\times A'$ with $|A'|=6$ has $36$ elements; $|S|=25$. So the conjecture fails.
* What the theorem gives instead: $\rho=\beta$ (on $P^*=\{(x,x)\}$), with cycles $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3,4,5\}$; the orbit bands are $\{1,2\}\times\{1,2\}$ and $\{3,4,5\}\times\{3,4,5\}$, on which $*$ and $+$ differ and $\phi$ has orders $2$ and $3$; $\operatorname{lcm}(2,3)=6=k$.

Any $\beta$ whose cycle type has lcm larger than every part, on a set too small to hold a $k\times k$ band, does the same (e.g. $\beta=(1\,2)(3\,4\,5)$ on $6$ points fixing $6$ gives $k=6$ with $|S|=36$, and then the whole $S$ is a band of the right size — so the size obstruction alone is not the full story; see 5.2).

## 5.2 Right size, but not flat

Take $S=B_2^0\cup B_3^0$, the $0$-direct union of a $2\times2$ and a $3\times3$ square band ($14$ elements; products across the two bands are $0$), with $*$ the swap on each band and $+$ the inversion twisted by $(1\,2)$ on $B_2$ and by $(3\,4\,5)$ on $B_3$. Then $k=6$, $\rho$ has cycles of lengths $2$ and $3$, and again there is no union of cycles of size $6$ (only $5$ projections).

To see the *other* failure mode, adjoin a third component, a $1\times1$ band $\{u\}$ with $u^*=u^+=u$ (or, as in the script, use $\mathcal M^0[\{1\};6,6;P]$ with $P$ block-diagonal with blocks of sizes $2,3,1$). Now the union $B$ of all cycles has size $6=k$ and $\rho|_B\ne\mathrm{id}$, but $\{ef:e,f\in B\}$ contains $0$ (products across components), so $B$ is not flat and there is no $*$,$+$-closed $6\times6$ band by Remark 3.5. The obstruction is no longer size — the $37$-element version has room for a $36$-element band — but the cross-orbit entries of $P$.

# 6. All inversions on a completely 0-simple regular $*$-semigroup

Setting as in Lemma A: $S=\mathcal M^0[G;I,I;P]$, $p_{ii}=1$, $p_{ji}=p_{ij}^{-1}$ (zeros symmetric), $(i,g,j)^*=(j,g^{-1},i)$, $e_i=(i,1,i)$. By Lemma B the inversions of $S$ correspond to permutations $\rho$ of $I$ with $p_{\rho i,i}\ne0$ satisfying $(\ast)$. Substituting $k=\rho^{-1}u$, $j=\rho v$ in $(\ast)$ and inverting, $(\ast)$ is equivalent to its **quadrilateral form**
$$p_{u,\rho v}\;=\;p_{u,\rho u}\;p_{\rho u,v}\;p_{v,\rho v}\qquad\text{for all }u,v\in I:\tag{Q}$$
the path $u\to\rho u\to v\to\rho v$ has the same holonomy as the edge $u\to\rho v$. Think of $P$ as a $G^0$-valued connection on the complete graph on $I$; a "gauge change" $p_{ij}\mapsto c_ip_{ij}c_j^{-1}$ ($c\colon I\to G$) is the Rees isomorphism $(i,g,j)\mapsto(i,c_igc_j^{-1},j)$, which preserves the normal form of Lemma A and the formula for $*$. A set $B\subseteq I$ is *flat* if all $p_{ij}$ ($i,j\in B$) are nonzero and $p_{ij}p_{jl}=p_{il}$ on $B$; equivalently $P|_{B\times B}$ is gauge-equivalent to the all-ones block.

For cycles $C,C'$ of $\rho$ with chosen base points $u\in C$, $v\in C'$, put
$$T_{u,v}(a,b)\;:=\;p_{u,\rho^au}\;p_{\rho^au,\rho^bv}\;p_{\rho^bv,v}\in G^0,$$
the holonomy of the path $u\to\rho^au\to\rho^bv\to v$ (well defined mod $|C|$ in $a$ and mod $|C'|$ in $b$ once $C,C'$ are flat).

## Theorem E

*Let $\rho$ be a permutation of $I$ with $p_{\rho i,i}\ne0$ for all $i$. The following are equivalent.*

1. *$\rho$ arises from an inversion of $S$, i.e. $(\ast)$ / (Q) holds.*
2. *Every $\rho$-cycle is flat, and for any two cycles $C,C'$ with base points $u,v$ the holonomy $T_{u,v}(a,b)$ depends only on $a+b$ (hence only on $a+b \bmod \gcd(|C|,|C'|)$).*
3. *After a gauge change, $p_{ij}=1$ whenever $i,j$ lie in the same $\rho$-cycle, and*
$$p_{\rho i,\,j}=p_{i,\,\rho j}\qquad\text{for all }i,j\in I,\tag{H}$$
*i.e. $P$ is constant on the orbits of $(i,j)\mapsto(\rho^{-1}i,\rho j)$ — which is precisely the action of $\phi$ on $\mathcal H$-classes. In these coordinates*
$$\phi(i,g,j)=(\rho^{-1}i,\ g,\ \rho j),\qquad (i,g,j)^+=(\rho^{-1}j,\ g^{-1},\ \rho i).$$

*Proof.* (1)⇒(2): cycles are flat by Lemma C. Fix $u\in C$, $v\in C'$. Using flatness on $C$ and $C'$ to expand $p_{u,\rho^{a+1}u}=p_{u,\rho^au}\,p_{\rho^au,\rho^{a+1}u}$ and $p_{\rho^bv,v}=p_{\rho^bv,\rho^{b+1}v}\,p_{\rho^{b+1}v,v}$, one sees that $T_{u,v}(a+1,b)=T_{u,v}(a,b+1)$ is exactly (Q) at the pair $(\rho^au,\rho^bv)$. So $T$ is constant along anti-diagonals: $T(a,b)=T(a+b,0)=T(0,a+b)$, and the first expression is periodic mod $|C|$, the second mod $|C'|$, in $a+b$.
(2)⇒(1): every pair $(u',v')$ is $(\rho^au,\rho^bv)$ for cycles' base points, and (Q) at $(u',v')$ is $T(a+1,b)=T(a,b+1)$, as just shown.
(2)⇔(3): take the gauge $c_i:=p_{ui}$ where $u$ is the base point of the cycle of $i$. Flatness gives $p_{ij}=p_{iu}p_{uj}=c_i^{-1}c_j$ on each cycle, so the new $p'_{ij}=c_ip_{ij}c_j^{-1}=1$ there; and $p'_{\rho^au,\rho^bv}=T_{u,v}(a,b)$ by definition, so (H) for $p'$ is $T(a+1,b)=T(a,b+1)$. Conversely, given (3), (Q) reads $p_{u,\rho v}=p_{\rho u,v}$, which is (H); and (Q) is invariant under gauge changes (it expresses that a certain map is an inversion). The formulas for $\phi$ and $+$ come from $(\dagger)$ and Lemma B with all $p_{i,\rho^{\pm1} i}=1$. $\square$

## Consequences and reformulations

**(a) A normal form for a pair of inversions.** In the coordinates of (3) both inversions are "in normal form at once": $*$ swaps rows and columns, $+$ swaps them and then relabels by $\rho$, and $\phi$ is the pure coordinate permutation $(i,g,j)\mapsto(\rho^{-1}i,g,\rho j)$. Condition (H) is exactly the condition that this coordinate permutation is an automorphism. So, conceptually:

> *A second inversion on $S$ is the same thing as a permutation $\rho$ of the index set $I$ such that, after a gauge change, the sandwich matrix is $\rho$-Hankel — $p_{\rho i,j}=p_{i,\rho j}$ — with all-ones blocks on the $\rho$-cycles. The inversion is distinct from $*$ iff $\rho\ne\mathrm{id}$.*

**(b) The free parameters.** Choose a partition of $I$ into cycles (i.e. $\rho$) and, for each ordered pair of distinct cycles $(C,C')$, a function
$$f_{CC'}\colon\ \mathbb Z/\gcd(|C|,|C'|)\longrightarrow G^0,\qquad f_{C'C}(n)=f_{CC'}(n)^{-1},$$
and set $p_{\rho^au,\rho^bv}=f_{CC'}(a+b)$, $p\equiv1$ on $C\times C$. Every such choice gives a completely 0-simple regular $*$-semigroup carrying the second inversion $\rho$, and every $(S,*,+)$ with a completely 0-simple $S$ arises this way. (Regularity of $P$ is automatic from $p_{ii}=1$.) The orbit bands of `03-main-theorem.md` are the diagonal blocks; the $f_{CC'}$ describe how they are glued. Note the gluing between cycles of coprime lengths is a single element of $G^0$.

**(c) Beyond rectangular groups.** The theorem produces completely simple regular $*$-semigroups with two inversions that are *not* orthodox. The smallest kind: $G=\mathbb Z_3=\langle t\rangle$, $I=\{u,\rho u,v,\rho v\}$ (two 2-cycles), $f_{CC'}(0)=1$, $f_{CC'}(1)=t$:
$$P=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1&t\\1&1&t&1\\1&t^{-1}&1&1\\t^{-1}&1&1&1\end{pmatrix},$$
which is not flat (the triangle $u,\rho u,v$ has holonomy $t$), so $S=\mathcal M[\mathbb Z_3;4,4;P]$ ($48$ elements) is not a rectangular group; it has exactly two inversions, $*$ and the one with $\rho=(u\ \rho u)(v\ \rho v)$, with $k=2$. The two $2\times2$ orbit bands sit inside a semigroup whose idempotents do not form a band.

**(d) Why $3$ rows gave "$6$ or $1$".** With $|I|=3$ any nontrivial $\rho$ is a transposition or a $3$-cycle. A $3$-cycle makes $I$ one flat cycle; a transposition $(u\ \rho u)$ fixing $w$ makes $\{u,\rho u\}$ flat, and (Q) at $(w,u)$ with $\rho w=w$ reads $p_{w,\rho u}=p_{ww}\,p_{wu}\,p_{u,\rho u}=p_{wu}\,p_{u,\rho u}$, so the triangle $\{w,u,\rho u\}$ is flat too. Either way $P$ is flat, $S\cong G\times(3\times3\text{ band})$, and all $3!$ permutations occur. For $|I|\ge4$ this collapse no longer happens, as (c) shows.

**(e) Structure of the set of inversions.** If $\rho$ is admissible so is every power $\rho^n$: in the coordinates of (3), $x\mapsto\phi^n(x^*)$ is $(i,g,j)\mapsto(\rho^{-n}j,g^{-1},\rho^ni)$, visibly an inversion. So the inversions of $S$ form a union of "cyclic families" $\{x\mapsto\phi^n(x^*)\}_{n}$ through $*$. They are **not** in general closed under the natural composition $\rho_1\rho_2$: e.g. two $3$-cycles glued by $f=(0,t,0)$ over $\mathbb Z_3$ admit $7$ permutations — the six that act on both cycles "in step" plus one that swaps the cycles — and composing the swap with the others leaves the admissible set. (This is in contrast to the square band, where all of $\mathrm{Sym}(I)$ occurs.)

**(f) Whole finite $S$.** Everything applies $\mathcal D$-class by $\mathcal D$-class: a second inversion on a finite regular $*$-semigroup is a family of admissible $\rho_D$ on the principal factors, with the constraint that the resulting anti-automorphism respects products between different $\mathcal D$-classes. The theorem above says nothing about that last constraint; that is where the non-completely-simple structure enters.

## Computer check (`verify2.py`)

For each of the following, the script (i) finds all inversions by brute force on the multiplication table, reads off $\rho$ from the $+$-projections, and confirms the set of $\rho$'s equals the set of permutations satisfying $(\ast)$; (ii) checks, for *every* permutation of $I$, that $(\ast)$ holds iff (2) holds; (iii) performs the gauge change of (3) and checks the all-ones cycle blocks and (H); (iv) checks closure under powers and tests closure under composition:

* the $4\times4$ example of (c) over $\mathbb Z_3$ — exactly $2$ inversions, $S$ not orthodox;
* six random "Hankel" constructions of (b) over $\mathbb Z_3$ and $S_3$ with cycle types $[3,3],[1,2,2],[2,2],[1,3]$, including ones with zero entries — the built-in $\rho$ is always admissible;
* all $27$ skew-symmetric $P$ over $\mathbb Z_3$ with $3$ rows.

All assertions pass; the $[3,3]$ example is the one exhibiting non-closure under composition in (e).

# 7. Global structure: inversions as twists of $*$ by permutations of $P^*$

$(S,*)$ is a finite regular $*$-semigroup, $P^*$ its set of projections. For a permutation $\rho$ of $P^*$ define
$$\phi_\rho(x):=\rho^{-1}(xx^*)\;x\;\rho(x^*x),\qquad x^{+_\rho}:=\phi_\rho(x^*)=\rho^{-1}(x^*x)\;x^*\;\rho(xx^*).$$

## Theorem G

1. *Every inversion $+$ of $S$ equals $+_\rho$ for exactly one permutation $\rho$ of $P^*$, namely $\rho(e)=e^{+*}e^{+}$; and then $\phi_\rho(x)=x^{*+}$ is the automorphism $\phi$ of the earlier sections.*
2. *Conversely, for a permutation $\rho$ of $P^*$, the map $+_\rho$ is an inversion if and only if $\phi_\rho$ is an automorphism of $S$.*

So the inversions of $(S,*)$ are in bijection with $\mathcal A(S,*):=\{\rho\in\mathrm{Sym}(P^*):\phi_\rho\in\mathrm{Aut}(S)\}$. In particular $|\mathrm{Inv}(S)|\le|\mathrm{Aut}(S)|$.

*Proof.* (1) Let $+$ be an inversion and $\rho(e)=e^{+*}e^+$. By Lemma B, on each $\mathcal D$-class $\rho$ is the permutation $e_i\mapsto e_{\rho i}$ of Lemma A's coordinates, and $(i,g,j)^+=(\rho^{-1}j,\ p_{\rho^{-1}j,j}\,g^{-1}\,p_{i,\rho i},\ \rho i)$ (since $q_j^{-1}=p_{\rho^{-1}j,j}$, $q_{\rho i}^{-1}=p_{i,\rho i}$). But for $x=(i,g,j)$ we have $x^*x=e_j$, $xx^*=e_i$, and
$$e_{\rho^{-1}j}\,(j,g^{-1},i)\,e_{\rho i}=(\rho^{-1}j,\ p_{\rho^{-1}j,j}\,g^{-1}\,p_{i,\rho i},\ \rho i),$$
so $x^+=x^{+_\rho}$. Uniqueness: $\rho$ is recovered from $+$ by the formula defining it. Replacing $x$ by $x^*$ gives $x^{*+}=\phi_\rho(x)$.

(2) Suppose $\phi:=\phi_\rho\in\mathrm{Aut}(S)$. Since $\phi(x)\in SxS$, $\phi$ moves every $\mathcal J$-class downwards; an automorphism permutes the finite poset of $\mathcal J$-classes, and a permutation of a finite poset with $\phi(J)\le J$ for all $J$ is the identity (each orbit is a descending cycle). So $\phi$ preserves $\mathcal D$-classes, hence $\rho^{-1}(e)\,e\,\rho(e)=\phi(e)\in D_e$, hence $\rho(e)\ge_{\mathcal J}e$ for all $e$, and the same cycle argument shows $\rho$ preserves $\mathcal D$-classes. In Lemma A's coordinates on $D^0$, $\phi(i,g,j)=e_{\rho^{-1}i}(i,g,j)e_{\rho j}=(\rho^{-1}i,\ p_{\rho^{-1}i,i}\,g\,p_{j,\rho j},\ \rho j)$, and $\phi(e_i)\in D$ forces $p_{\rho^{-1}i,i}\ne0\ne p_{i,\rho i}$. Now $x\mapsto\phi(x^*)$ is an anti-automorphism, and
$$x\,\phi(x^*)\,x=(i,\ g\,p_{j,\rho^{-1}j}\,p_{\rho^{-1}j,j}\,g^{-1}\,p_{i,\rho i}\,p_{\rho i,i}\,g,\ j)=(i,g,j)=x,$$
$$\phi(x^*)^*=\big(\rho^{-1}(x^*x)\,x^*\,\rho(xx^*)\big)^*=\rho(xx^*)\,x\,\rho^{-1}(x^*x)=\phi_{\rho^{-1}}(x),$$
and $\phi_{\rho^{-1}}\phi_\rho=\mathrm{id}$ by the coordinate formula, so $x^{++}=\phi(\phi_{\rho^{-1}}(x))=x$. Thus $+_\rho$ is an inversion; its associated permutation is $e\mapsto e^{+*}e^{+}=e_{\rho i}e_ie_{\rho^{-1}i}e_ie_{\rho i}=e_{\rho i}=\rho(e)$. Conversely, if $+_\rho$ is an inversion then $\phi_\rho(x)=x^{*+_\rho}$ is an automorphism. $\square$

## Reducing "$\phi_\rho\in\mathrm{Aut}(S)$" to local and gluing conditions

**Lemma H.** *Let $S$ be a regular semigroup and $\phi\colon S\to S$ a map with $\phi(ab)=\phi(a)\phi(b)$ whenever (i) $ab\,\mathcal R\,a$, or (ii) $ab\,\mathcal L\,b$, or (iii) $a,b$ are idempotents (projections suffice in a regular $*$-semigroup). Then $\phi$ is an endomorphism.*

*Proof.* Given $a,b$, pick $a'\in V(a)$, $b'\in V(b)$ (in the $*$-case $a'=a^*$, $b'=b^*$) and put $f=a'a$, $e=bb'$; then $af=a$, $eb=b$, $ab=(afe)b$. One checks $afe\,\mathcal L\,fe$ (as $fe=a'(afe)$), $(afe)b\,\mathcal R\,afe$ (as $afe=ae=(ab)b'$), $af\,\mathcal L\,f$ and $eb\,\mathcal R\,e$. Hence $\phi(ab)\overset{(i)}=\phi(afe)\phi(b)\overset{(ii)}=\phi(a)\phi(fe)\phi(b)\overset{(iii)}=\phi(a)\phi(f)\phi(e)\phi(b)\overset{(ii),(i)}=\phi(af)\phi(eb)=\phi(a)\phi(b)$. $\square$

**Theorem G′.** *For a permutation $\rho$ of $P^*$, $\phi_\rho\in\mathrm{Aut}(S)$ iff*

* *(L) $\rho$ preserves each $\mathcal D$-class and is admissible on each principal factor in the sense of Theorem E (equivalently, $\phi_\rho$ restricts to an automorphism of every $D^0$); and*
* *(G) $\phi_\rho(ab)=\phi_\rho(a)\phi_\rho(b)$ for all pairs $a,b$ from different $\mathcal D$-classes with $ab\,\mathcal R\,a$ or $ab\,\mathcal L\,b$, and for all pairs of projections $a,b$.*

*Proof.* (L) makes $\phi_\rho$ a bijection of $S$ and multiplicative on pairs $a,b\in D$ with $ab\in D$ (then $ab\,\mathcal R\,a$ by stability); together with (G) all pairs of Lemma H are covered. $\square$

**The gluing condition in Schützenberger form.** Fix $D$ with the coordinates of Theorem E for $\rho|_D$, so $\phi_\rho(i,g,j)=(\rho^{-1}i,g,\rho j)$. For $s\in S$, left multiplication by $s$ acts on $D^0$ by a row-monomial matrix: $s\cdot(i,g,j)=(s\cdot i,\ \omega_i(s)\,g,\ j)$ or $0$, with $s\cdot i\in I$ and $\omega_i(s)\in G$ independent of $g,j$ (Green's lemma). Multiplicativity of $\phi=\phi_\rho$ on pairs $(s,x)$, $x\in D$, $sx\in D$, reads
$$\rho^{-1}(s\cdot i)=\phi(s)\cdot\rho^{-1}(i),\qquad \omega_i(s)=\omega_{\rho^{-1}i}(\phi(s)),$$
i.e. the row representation of $\phi(s)$ on $D$ is the conjugate by $\rho$ of that of $s$; dually for columns. In particular, **if $\phi(s)=s$ then the row and column representations of $s$ on every $\mathcal D$-class commute with $\rho$.** This applies to every $s$ in a $\mathcal D$-class on which $\rho$ is trivial — for instance every $\mathcal D$-class consisting of a single $\mathcal H$-class (the group of units of a monoid, any $\mathcal D$-class that is a group): $\phi_\rho(i,g,i)=(i,g,i)$ there, since $|I|=1$ forces $\rho=\mathrm{id}$.

## A family where the gluing condition is everything

Let $H\le\mathrm{Sym}(A)$ act on a set $A$ and let $S=H\cup(A\times A)$ with $H$ a group, $A\times A$ the square band, and
$$h\,(a,b)=(ha,\,b),\qquad (a,b)\,h=(a,\,h^{-1}b).$$
This is a semigroup (the band is an ideal), and $h^*=h^{-1}$, $(a,b)^*=(b,a)$ is an inversion. Locally there is no constraint: the band admits all $|A|!$ permutations of its projections $\{(a,a)\}$, and $H$ admits only the identity. Globally, Theorem G says $\rho\leftrightarrow\alpha\in\mathrm{Sym}(A)$ works iff $\phi_\rho(a,b)=(\alpha^{-1}a,\alpha b)$ is compatible with the action: $\phi(h(a,b))=(\alpha^{-1}ha,\alpha b)$ must equal $h\,\phi(a,b)=(h\alpha^{-1}a,\alpha b)$. Hence
$$\mathrm{Inv}(S)\;\longleftrightarrow\;C_{\mathrm{Sym}(A)}(H).$$
For $|A|=3$: $H=1,\ \langle(01)\rangle,\ \langle(012)\rangle,\ \mathrm{Sym}(3)$ give $6,2,3,1$ inversions (verified by brute force). So the number of inversions is not determined by the principal factors, and no gluing condition weaker than "$\phi_\rho$ is an automorphism" can be expected in general: the answer here is a centraliser in $\mathrm{Aut}(S)$.

## Further consequences

* **Closure properties.** $\mathcal A(S,*)$ is closed under powers ($\phi_{\rho}^n=\phi_{\rho^n}$, using flatness of orbits), hence under inverses, and under conjugation by automorphisms of $S$ commuting with $*$. It is not closed under composition in general (§6(e)).
* **$k$ and the orbit bands** (Theorem of §3) are now statements about $\rho\in\mathcal A(S,*)$: $k=\operatorname{ord}(\rho)$, and each cycle $(e_0\cdots e_{m-1})$ of $\rho$ carries the band $\{e_ae_b\}$.
* **$\rho$ preserves the natural order, but not the projection algebra.** Recall the natural order on $P^*$: $e\le f$ iff $e=ef=fe$. *Every admissible $\rho$ is an automorphism of the poset $(P^*,\le)$.* Indeed, let $e\le f$ and put $u=e^+$, $v=f^+$. Applying the anti-automorphism $+$ to $e=ef=fe$ gives $u=vu=uv$, and applying $*$ gives $u^*=v^*u^*=u^*v^*$. With $\rho(e)=u^*u$, $\rho(f)=v^*v$:
$$\rho(e)\rho(f)=u^*(uv)v^*v=u^*u\,(vv^*v)=u^*uv=u^*u=\rho(e),\qquad
\rho(f)\rho(e)=v^*v\,(v^*u^*)u=(v^*vv^*)u^*u=(uv)^*u=u^*u=\rho(e),$$
so $\rho(e)\le\rho(f)$. Since $\rho^{-1}$ is the permutation attached to the inversion ${*}{+}{*}$, it is order-preserving too. By contrast, $\rho$ need **not** preserve the projection-algebra operation $e\mapsto fef$: in $\mathcal M^0[\mathbb Z_3;6,6;P]$ with two $3$-cycles glued by $f=(0,t,0)$, two of the seven admissible $\rho$ fail $\rho(fef)=\rho(f)\rho(e)\rho(f)$, because they change the zero pattern of $P$ — (H) only forces $p_{\rho i,\rho j}=p_{i,\rho^2j}$. The two facts are compatible: distinct projections in one $\mathcal D$-class are incomparable, so the order lives entirely between $\mathcal D$-classes (where $\rho$ behaves), while $fef$ still detects whether $p_{ij}=0$ inside a $\mathcal D$-class (where $\rho$ may scramble it). Numerically checked on $(5\times5\text{ band})^1$, $H\cup(3\times3)$ and the $\mathcal M^0$ example above.
* **Uniqueness.** $S$ has a unique inversion iff $\mathcal A(S,*)=\{\mathrm{id}\}$; a nontrivial $\rho$ needs a $\mathcal D$-class with a flat $\rho$-cycle of length $\ge2$ (Lemma C) whose Schützenberger representations are $\rho$-equivariant. Two simple sufficient conditions for uniqueness: every $\mathcal D$-class is a single $\mathcal H$-class (semilattices of groups); or $S$ is a monoid whose unit group acts on the projections of every $\mathcal D$-class with trivial centraliser in $\mathrm{Sym}(P^*\cap D)$ (as $\mathrm{Sym}(3)$ does in the family above).

* **Unique inversion does not mean inverse.** If $S$ is not inverse, some $x$ has $|V(x)|>1$, so there are always maps $x\mapsto x'\in V(x)$ other than $*$; but such a broad-sense inversion need not be involutory or antiautomorphic, and its existence says nothing about a second inversion in our sense. Indeed every $\mathcal M[\mathbb Z_3;3,3;P]$ with non-flat $P$, and $\mathrm{Sym}(3)\cup(3\times3\text{ band})$ above, have exactly one inversion without being inverse. The smallest example is a **5-element band**: $S=\{s\}\cup(\{0,1\}\times\{0,1\})$ with $s^2=s$, $s(a,b)=(0,b)$, $(a,b)s=(a,0)$, and $s^*=s$, $(a,b)^*=(b,a)$. It is a band with two $\mathcal D$-classes and non-commuting idempotents, so not inverse; a second inversion would need the swap of $\{0,1\}$ to commute with the row action of $s$, which is the constant map $0$ — impossible. (Brute force: one inversion, and $|V(x)|=4$ for each of the four band elements, as in any rectangular band.) Nothing smaller works: the only non-inverse regular $*$-semigroup of order $\le4$ is the $2\times2$ band, which has two inversions.

## Summary: the structure theory

A pair of inversions on a finite regular $*$-semigroup is the same as a pair $\big((S,*),\rho\big)$ with $\rho$ a permutation of the projection set such that $\phi_\rho\colon x\mapsto\rho^{-1}(xx^*)\,x\,\rho(x^*x)$ is an automorphism. This splits into:

1. **local data** — on each $\mathcal D$-class, a partition into $\rho$-cycles and a $\rho$-Hankel sandwich matrix with all-ones cycle blocks (Theorem E); the cycles are the orbit bands and $k=\operatorname{ord}(\rho)$;
2. **gluing data** — equivariance of the Schützenberger representations under $\rho$ (Theorem G′ / Lemma H), a condition that genuinely depends on the whole of $S$ and can cut the local count down to $1$.

## Computer check (`verify3.py`)

For square bands, the $14$-element $0$-direct union, the four $H\cup(3\times3)$ semigroups, the non-orthodox $\mathcal M[\mathbb Z_3;4,4;P]$ and the $109$-element $\mathcal M^0[\mathbb Z_3;6,6;P]$: every brute-force inversion equals $+_\rho$ for $\rho(e)=e^{+*}e^+$; for every permutation $\rho$ of $P^*$ (when $|P^*|!$ is small), "$+_\rho$ is an inversion" ⟺ "$\phi_\rho$ is an automorphism" ⟺ "$\phi_\rho$ is bijective and multiplicative on the pairs of Lemma H"; the counts for $H\cup(3\times3)$ equal $|C_{\mathrm{Sym}(3)}(H)|$; and the projection-algebra failure is exhibited. All assertions pass.

# 8. Rigid regular $*$-semigroups (unique inversion)

Call $(S,*)$ **rigid** if $*$ is its only inversion. Inverse semigroups are rigid; §7 shows the converse fails already for a 5-element band. This section collects what the structure theory says about rigidity, and an honest assessment of how much more there is to say.

## 8.1 What rigidity is

By Theorem G, $S$ is rigid iff $\mathcal A(S,*)=\{\mathrm{id}\}$: no non-identity permutation $\rho$ of $P^*$ makes $x\mapsto\rho^{-1}(xx^*)\,x\,\rho(x^*x)$ an automorphism. So rigidity is the absence of a certain kind of symmetry. Two layers:

* **locally rigid**: every principal factor $D^0$ is rigid, i.e. no $\mathcal D$-class admits a non-identity $\rho$ satisfying $(\ast)$ (equivalently, by Theorem E, no $\mathcal D$-class has a partition into $\rho$-cycles with all-ones blocks and a $\rho$-Hankel sandwich matrix after a gauge change, other than the trivial one);
* **rigid**: no non-identity $\rho$ is admissible globally.

Locally rigid $\Rightarrow$ rigid (a non-identity admissible $\rho$ is non-identity on some $\mathcal D$-class). The converse fails: the 5-element band $T$ is rigid but its band $\mathcal D$-class is the $2\times2$ band, which has two inversions. Note also that a $\mathcal D$-class with exactly two $\mathcal R$-classes is never locally rigid (a $2\times2$ block is always flat), so a rigid $S$ with such a $\mathcal D$-class is rigid only because of the gluing.

## 8.2 Rigidity is generic (completely simple case)

**Proposition.** *Let $|I|=n\ge3$ and $G$ a finite group. Among the $|G|^{\binom n2}$ sandwich matrices $P$ in the normal form of Lemma A (unit diagonal, $p_{ji}=p_{ij}^{-1}$), the fraction for which $\mathcal M[G;I,I;P]$ is not rigid is at most $(n!-1)/|G|$.* For $n=2$ the semigroup is never rigid; for $n=1$ it is a group.

*Proof.* Fix a non-identity permutation $\rho$ of $I$ and $u$ with $\rho u\ne u$. If $\rho^2u\ne u$, condition (Q) at $(u,\rho u)$ reads $p_{u,\rho^2u}=p_{u,\rho u}\,p_{\rho u,\rho^2u}$, an equation between three distinct edges of the complete graph on $I$. If $\rho^2u=u$, pick $v\notin\{u,\rho u\}$; (Q) at $(u,v)$ reads $p_{u,\rho v}=p_{u,\rho u}\,p_{\rho u,v}\,p_{v,\rho v}$, and the edge $\{u,\rho v\}$ differs from the other three edges appearing. In either case the equation determines one entry of $P$ uniquely in terms of others, so at most $|G|^{\binom n2-1}$ matrices satisfy it. Summing over the $n!-1$ non-identity $\rho$ gives the bound. $\square$

For $n=3$ the exact answer is $1-1/|G|$ (rigid iff $P$ is not flat, §6(d)); checked numerically for $G=\mathbb Z_2,\mathbb Z_3,\mathbb Z_5,\mathbb Z_7$. So among completely simple regular $*$-semigroups with at least three $\mathcal R$-classes, rigidity is the rule and a second inversion the exception — the opposite of the intuition one might get from bands.

## 8.3 Closure properties

* $S^0$ and $S^1$ are rigid iff $S$ is: the new element is alone in its $\mathcal D$-class, so any admissible $\rho$ fixes it, and $\phi_\rho$ is an automorphism of $S^0$ (resp. $S^1$) iff its restriction is one of $S$.
* Direct products: in the tested cases ($T\times T$, $T\times M$, $M\times M$ with $M=\mathcal M[\mathbb Z_2;3,3;P]$ rigid) the product of rigid semigroups is rigid. I do not have a proof: $\mathcal D$-classes of $S_1\times S_2$ are products $D_1\times D_2$, and a permutation of $P_1^*\times P_2^*$ need not be a product of permutations (for $B_2\times B_2\cong 4\times4$ band there are $24$ admissible $\rho$ but only $4$ product ones), so the argument would have to use the gluing. This is a natural open question.
* Not closed under subsemigroups or $*$-subsemigroups ($B_2\subset T$), nor under $*$-homomorphic images (every $\mathcal M[G;I,I;P]$ maps onto the square band $I\times I$, which has $|I|!$ inversions), nor under ideals in general.

So rigidity is not a variety- or quasivariety-like property, and it is not inherited in either direction. This is the main reason I would not expect a structure theory of rigid regular $*$-semigroups that goes beyond the general theory.

## 8.4 Assessment

The situation seems to be exactly parallel to *asymmetric graphs* (graphs with trivial automorphism group): by Theorem G, inversions are certain automorphisms of $S$, so "unique inversion" is a "no symmetry" condition; such conditions are generic (§8.2), non-hereditary (§8.3), and have no structure theory of their own — the structure theory is that of the ambient class, plus a symmetry computation. What the present notes add is that the symmetry computation is completely explicit: rigidity of $S$ is decided by (a) the $\rho$-Hankel analysis of each sandwich matrix (Theorem E) and (b) the equivariance of the Schützenberger representations (Theorem G′). In particular:

* if every $\mathcal D$-class of $S$ is locally rigid, $S$ is rigid, and this is a condition on sandwich matrices alone;
* if $S$ is a monoid and, for every $\mathcal D$-class $D$, the group of units acts on $P^*\cap D$ with trivial centraliser in $\mathrm{Sym}(P^*\cap D)$, then $S$ is rigid (§7);
* the only way for a *band* $\mathcal D$-class (or any $\mathcal D$-class with a flat cycle) to sit inside a rigid $S$ is through such gluing constraints, as in the 5-element example.

Where a deeper theory might exist is for the *intermediate* class of locally rigid semigroups, since local rigidity is a property of the principal factors alone and behaves better under the usual constructions (for instance it is inherited by ideals, whose principal factors are principal factors of $S$, and it passes to $S^0$ and $S^1$). Even there, though, the content reduces to a statement about sandwich matrices: no cycle of any permutation of the rows is flat and compatible in the sense of Theorem E. I would be happy to be proved wrong by a more clever invariant, but I do not see one.

Confirms: the 5-element band has a unique inversion and every band element has two inverses; $T\times T$, $T\times M$, $M\times M$ are rigid; and the rigid fraction of 3-row sandwich matrices over $\mathbb Z_q$ is exactly $1-1/q$ for $q=2,3,5,7$.
